## When a tool stops being outside us

There are technologies that, at first, seem to remain in front of us.

A screen. A text box. An answer appearing with almost indecent speed. Something we use and then close. Something external, obedient, apparently separate from life.

But not every tool stays outside.

Some begin to enter the texture of experience itself: the way we search for an idea, the way we tolerate doubt, the patience with which we read, the way we write a sentence before we know exactly what we are trying to say. They enter, above all, that less visible territory where a person decides whether to sustain the effort of understanding or accept the first available form of relief.

Artificial intelligence is already entering that place.

That is why it feels poor to speak about AI only as innovation, threat, or promise. Those words have a strange comfort. They let us believe that the future has already been decided somewhere else, by companies, models, markets, or laboratories. As if technology advanced over us with an impersonal necessity and all that remained for us was to adapt, obey, or complain.

But the everyday experience of AI is more ambiguous and more intimate.

Sometimes it appears as real help: it clarifies an idea, opens a possibility, reduces the friction between an intuition and a first form. Other times it appears as a silent temptation: not to think all the way through, not to read all the way through, not to learn all the way through, not to decide all the way through. The same tool can feel like an extension of our capacity or like a minimal, almost invisible surrender of it.

So the question is not only how powerful AI becomes.

The question is what kind of consciousness we form around it.

AI does not automatically drag us toward a good or bad future. It amplifies the posture from which we use it. It can become another layer of dependency, where we think less, build less, and hand over more of our practical life to systems we do not understand or control. Or it can become a layer of capability, where more people learn, create, automate, design, and solve problems that used to be out of reach.

The fork is not only in the technology.

It is in the relationship we establish with it.

## The bad fork: dependency

Dependency does not always arrive violently.

Sometimes it arrives as convenience.

You begin by using AI to save time. Then to avoid a boring task. Then to avoid facing a blank page. Later, to avoid ordering an idea. And one day, perhaps without drama, an uncomfortable question appears: am I using this tool to think better, or so I do not have to think?

That is the first risk: not that AI becomes powerful, but that we become weaker around it.

Not suddenly ignorant. Not incapable overnight. Rather, less willing to sustain certain efforts: doubt, slow reading, the frustration of not understanding, the need to test a sentence until it says what we really meant, the patient work of forming our own judgment.

AI can help us with all of that.

It can also anesthetize it.

When an answer appears too quickly, something in thought can remain unused. When a text comes out too clean, something of the voice can be left outside. When a fragment of code works without being understood, something in the system remains dark. And what remains dark eventually becomes dependency.

There is another dependency, less intimate but just as decisive. Many AI tools live inside closed platforms. We use models we do not control, under rules that can change, with prices that can move, with limits we do not always understand, and with data whose path we do not always see clearly.

For a person, that can be inconvenient.

For an organization, it can be risky.

For a region, it can become a silent form of technological subordination.

In Latin America we know that feeling well: using systems that were not designed from our context, adapting our institutions to external tools, translating our needs into categories made for other markets, depending on infrastructures that continue to work only as long as someone else decides they should.

AI can intensify that history.

It can make us faster, but not necessarily more autonomous. It can give us more output, but not necessarily more understanding. It can multiply what is visible while slowly reducing the invisible capacity that made the work ours.

A person can write more and still have less voice. They can generate more code and understand less architecture. They can receive more answers and formulate poorer questions.

That is the most subtle danger: not a humanity destroyed by machines, but a humanity that, through comfort, stops exercising essential parts of itself.

## The good fork: capability

But another path exists.

AI can also appear differently: not as a replacement for consciousness, but as an expansion of action.

There are moments when a tool does not replace us, but returns movement to us. It lets us begin where we used to remain stuck. It gives a first structure to an idea that still has no contour. It shows an alternative. It forces us to formulate a better question. It lets us look at a problem from an angle we had not considered.

In that sense, AI can become a layer of capability.

Not because it eliminates effort. That is a poor fantasy. Truly useful tools do not remove the need for judgment; they make it more important. What they do is move the border of the possible. They help a person arrive sooner at the point where they can decide, edit, compare, correct, build.

A developer can prototype an idea with less friction. A student can receive another explanation when the first one was not enough. A small business can automate a task that used to consume hours. A designer can explore directions before committing to one. A bilingual person can move more easily between languages, audiences, and contexts. A small institution can begin creating internal systems without always waiting for an external provider.

None of that is magic.

But it matters.

For a long time, building technology required a difficult mixture of time, training, money, and access. AI does not erase those barriers, but it can make them less absolute. It can bring more people closer to the first draft, the first prototype, the first working system, the first version of an idea that might otherwise have remained shapeless.

The difference is posture.

It is not the same to say, "do it for me," as it is to say, "help me understand it." It is not the same to copy an answer as it is to use it as material for thought. It is not the same to produce content to fill space as it is to take an initial structure and then submit it to experience, intention, and voice.

Capability does not appear when we let AI do everything.

It appears when we use it to do better what we still want to understand.

## What decides the path

The fork between dependency and capability will not be decided only by better models.

A faster model does not guarantee a healthier relationship with technology. A cleaner interface does not guarantee more autonomy. A more powerful tool can expand human capability, but it can also deepen passivity if it enters a culture that rewards only speed, quantity, and the appearance of productivity.

What decides the path is at another level.

It is in education, if it teaches us to use AI to ask better questions and not only to deliver answers. It is in ownership, if we accept renting all our technical capacity or if we build something we can understand and modify. It is in local context, if we use these tools to solve real problems instead of only imitating external trends. It is in human review, if we still defend a space where judgment, taste, and responsibility are not delegated. It is in builder culture, if we use AI to consume more or to create better.

The question "should we use AI?" has already become too small.

The important questions are more uncomfortable.

> What part of my thinking am I strengthening with this tool?  
> What part am I no longer practicing?  
> Who controls the infrastructure I depend on?  
> What can I build locally instead of only consuming?  
> Where should human judgment remain?

This is not about rejecting AI. Rejecting it completely would be unrealistic and, in many cases, not very useful.

It is about not accepting it while asleep.

## Why this matters for El Salvador and LATAM

In El Salvador and Latin America, this conversation should not feel distant.

Sometimes we talk about AI as if it belonged to another world: enormous labs, giant companies, data centers, investors, elite universities, Silicon Valley. But the real experience of technology does not happen only there. It also happens in a small office still depending on fragile spreadsheets, in a school where a student is trying to understand something without enough guidance, in a family business losing hours to repetitive work, in a public institution that needs to organize its information better, in an independent developer trying to build something of their own with limited resources.

There, AI can matter a lot.

The risk is that it arrives only as consumption. Another subscription. Another external product. Another tool built for other markets, with other assumptions, inside another mental language, which we simply learn to use while the real capacity stays elsewhere.

But the opportunity is also concrete: using AI to build more from here.

Not necessarily huge platforms. Not necessarily startups with enormous speeches. Sometimes capability appears in small systems: an automation that saves hours, an internal application that organizes a process, a guide that makes something confusing understandable, a bilingual tool, a personal website, a clearer database, a more humane way to access information.

That kind of technology rarely looks spectacular.

But it changes the real capacity of the people who use it.

That is why one question feels central to our region: can we use AI so more people can build tools of their own?

Not only to consume faster. Not only to publish more. Not only to look modern. But to solve concrete problems, learn useful skills, and reduce dependency.

That is where AI stops being a trend and starts becoming infrastructure for capability.

## The position I want to build from

This reflection also explains why I want to write and build more in public.

I am interested in small tools, personal systems, automation, interfaces, digital products, and workflows that make a person or a team more capable. Not because technology is salvation. It is not. Technology can distract, centralize, surveil, exploit, homogenize, and make us dependent.

That is exactly why it deserves a more careful gaze.

It is not enough to adopt new tools. We have to ask what they produce in us when they become habit. What kind of attention they form. What kind of work they make easier. What kind of dependency they introduce. What kind of capability they awaken or put to sleep.

In my case, I want to use AI as a support layer, not as a replacement for judgment.

I want it to help generate drafts, but not decide what is worth publishing. To help explore code, but not avoid understanding it. To help design options, but not replace taste. To help automate friction, but not erase responsibility. To help build more, not think less.

That is the position I want to build from: AI as capability, not dependency.

## The future is still open

AI will keep advancing.

That seems clear.

What is not clear is what kind of humanity, what kind of work, and what kind of societies we will form around it.

We can use it to produce more generic content, depend more on closed platforms, and let others define the shape of our digital future. Or we can use it to learn faster, build local tools, expand our creativity, and become more capable of solving real problems.

The difference is not only in the technology.

It is in the relationship we build with it.

The bad future is not simply one where AI thinks. It is one where we stop practicing thought. The good future is not one where AI does everything. It is one where it helps us think, create, and build with more force.

That fork is still open.

And that is exactly why it is worth talking about now.
